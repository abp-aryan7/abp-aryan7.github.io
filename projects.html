The Idea

After watching Ben Makes Everything create a laser projector on his YouTube channel, I became inspired to create my own; however, I wanted to see if I could simplify his approach.

The goal was to scan a laser across a surface so fast that it would create an image. At first, I was going to use two stepper motors to angle the beam to its desired destination, but I soon followed Ben Makes Everything with the use of an old hard drive motor.

The electronics side had its own learning curve. Early on, I damaged a stepper motor driver and an Arduino Uno by feeding them the wrong voltage. Each time I messed up, I had to fix a mechanical issue. This iteration felt more like tuning an instrument rather than just fixing some code.

02

The Story

Question that started it: Can I recreate a projector with a laser?

When I first saw Ben Makes Everything's video of laser projectors drawing shapes in midair, I was fascinated by how something as simple as a single beam of light could produce detailed animations and images. I understood the basic idea that mirrors could redirect the beam, but I had no real understanding of how those mirrors were controlled with enough precision to create recognizable patterns. Rather than buying a ready-made projector, I wanted to build one from scratch so I could understand every part of the system.

My initial design centered around an idea that was simple and based on a laser show machine. I used two NEMA 17 stepper motors that were aligned perpendicular to each other. They both had a mirror on them that would reflect the laser beam to create shapes. At first, I had the beam scanning back and forth like a Raster Scan in old televisions; however, I soon realized that for the design, this was inefficient. Since I wanted to create similar images as Ben Makes Everything, I would have to redesign the entire mechanism.

The next design I created used an old hard drive from a computer I found lying around in my house. While this design did create the projection I was expecting, it was not as efficient as I was hoping. Since I did not have a motor driver for the hard drive motor, I used the PCB that came in the hard drive; however, this PCB had a limit of 15 seconds before it would turn the hard drive motor off. Alongside this time limit, creating the 12-facet polygon mirror was harder than it first seemed.

Because the mirrors were incredibly small, even tiny imperfections in their mounting produced noticeable distortions in the projected image. I designed and repeatedly redesigned 3D-printed mirror holders, experimenting with different ways of keeping the mirrors perfectly flat and aligned. I finally ended on a design that firmly held the mirrors flat to the body of the polygon. After this issue was resolved, I noticed that there was a slight tilt to the projected image due to the location of the laser and mirrors.

In the next rendition, I added a BLDC motor driver so that the image could be projected for longer. I also switched out the laser for a TTL laser pointer. This laser pointer was better since it would be faster at turning on and off compared to a 532 nm Green Diode Laser. One thing I was still noticing was that the image that was projected was tilted to the left. I traced this issue back to the positioning of the laser. In the final design, I centered the laser so that the distortion would be centered and easier to fix.

Programming the projector became another opportunity to connect theory with reality. I wrote Arduino code to coordinate the laser, control the motors, and calibrate their movement using sensors. I designed the code so that it would project a 12 x 12 pixel grid. Using this layout, I created letters that I could use to project my name.

One of my favorite moments came when the projector finally began producing recognizable patterns. Although the images were far from perfect, watching a design emerge from nothing more than a moving laser beam and two rotating mirrors was incredibly rewarding. After weeks of troubleshooting alignment issues, redesigning printed parts, and refining the control software, seeing the system behave as intended made every iteration worthwhile.

Even now, I don't consider the project finished. I plan to improve the projection quality by refining the mirror mounts, increasing the scanning speed, and implementing more advanced control algorithms that produce smoother, more accurate images. I'd also like to explore features such as grayscale rendering, color projection, and eventually projecting simple animations. Every improvement opens the door to another engineering challenge, which is exactly what makes the project so rewarding.

More than anything, this project taught me that successful engineering depends on bringing together many different disciplines rather than mastering just one. Mechanical tolerances, electrical circuits, embedded programming, and optics all had to work together before the system could succeed. It reinforced something I've come to appreciate throughout my engineering projects: understanding how individual components work is important, but understanding how they interact is what ultimately turns an idea into a functioning system.

Results

After calibrating the system and refining the mechanics and control, the projector is able to scan recognizable letters using a single laser, spelling out my own name one test pattern at a time.

Electronics

An Arduino Nano acts as the central controller, generating the step pulses and timing the laser so the beam only fires where it needs to. Each axis has its own motor and driver:

  • Arduino Nano: coordinates both axes and switches the TTL laser diode on and off in sync with the scan
  • NEMA 17 stepper motor driven by an A4988 stepper driver: controls the Y-axis
  • Salvaged HDD spindle motor driven by a BLDC motor controller: spins the 12-facet polygon mirror for the X-axis
The Arduino Nano and motor driver wiring that coordinates the laser and both axes.
05

What's Next

The projector reliably scans recognizable letters, but the image still shows some distortion, largely a result of the accuracy limits of a 3D-printed 12-facet polygon mirror, which is difficult to manufacture with enough precision on a consumer 3D printer. A few upgrades are next on the list:

Precision Mirror

A more precisely manufactured polygon mirror to reduce the distortion introduced by the current 3D-printed part.

Galvanometer Upgrade

Replacing the NEMA 17 stepper motor with a galvanometer for faster, more precise beam deflection than a stepper can provide.

Multi-Color Laser

A multi-color laser module to improve image fidelity and enable full-color projections.

The Idea

Instead of accepting that a computer "just works," I wanted to understand every layer beneath the software. For example, I wanted to learn how the clock circuit interacts with the control logic that decides what happens on every cycle.

Following Ben Eater's architecture, I built and wired every module myself to see how instructions actually move through hardware. Many of the chips the build needed were hard to find or overpriced locally. Because of this issue, I sourced several while visiting family in India.

The finished computer runs real programs, but I still think of it as a foundation rather than a finished project. The next additions on my list are more memory, a simple display, and eventually a game like Snake running on hand-soldered logic.

The Story

Question that started it: How does a computer actually execute instructions?

When I first began coding, I became very interested in understanding what happens inside the computer that allowed it to read and process my code. At first, I came across different videos that explained binary and how it is used to create the logic behind computers. Soon, I came across Ben Eater's video series showing how to make a Breadboard 8-bit Computer. Instantly, I knew that I wanted to follow this tutorial to learn how a computer functions.

One challenge I ran into was simply obtaining the parts for the computer. Many of the chips required for the project were difficult to find or surprisingly expensive. Since I had the project in mind when I was visiting family in India, I took advantage of the opportunity to purchase many of the components at a lower price while in India. While this approach did work, many of the integrated circuits were different than the ones Ben Eater used in his videos. This mismatch led me to research and read the datasheets for each chip to learn the purpose of the pins. Looking back, this detour helped me realize that a project doesn't start with the assembly; it starts with the planning, budgeting, and ideation stages.

Although the overall architecture of my 8-bit computer followed Ben Eater's design, my goal extended far beyond assembling the computer. My true goal was to understand how the different parts of the computer interacted with each other and worked. I wanted to understand the purpose of each wire involved. Slowly, I constructed the clock module, registers, arithmetic logic unit (ALU), RAM, and control logic and learned how they interacted with one another.

One of my favorite moments during the project was watching concepts that had once existed only in videos come to life. It was very interesting seeing the control logic, clock unit, and ALU work together in a circuit I could see in real time. This visualization helped me understand how fast data travels through a computer to perform even simple tasks such as counting.

This project not only taught me about computer architecture, but I also developed my skills in chip debugging. Throughout the process of creating the 8-bit computer, I had to constantly look for loose connections and malfunctioning chips. The process also taught me a lot about analyzing datasheets. Since I was using some chips Ben Eater did not have in his design, I had to learn how to integrate those chips into the architecture.

Although the computer successfully executes programs, I still don't consider the project finished. If I continue developing it, I'd love to expand its capabilities by adding new instructions, increasing available memory, and connecting a simple display. One idea that particularly excites me is modifying the computer to run simple games such as Tetris or Snake. Overall, this project changed the way I think about computers. While the modules seemed complex at first, after I broke them down, I started to understand how a computer truly operates.

02

The Story

Question that started it: Can I recreate the motion of the Solar System using physics?

Long before starting this project, I had attempted to build a solar system simulation of my own. Although I was proud of what I created at the time, I knew it wasn't an accurate representation of how gravity actually works. The simulation relied on many shortcuts, and I lacked both the physics knowledge and programming experience to model planetary motion realistically.

Everything changed when I began learning about Newtonian physics while at Georgia Tech. I found myself wondering if those same equations could be used to fix my model of the solar system. I saw it as an opportunity to revisit one of my earlier projects and rebuild it using a much deeper understanding of both physics and programming.

First, I coded a small simulation that included the four rocky planets and the Sun. While this simulation was simple, it was a more accurate recreation of my older solar system simulation. While I had already reached my initial goal, I wanted to add the outer planets of the solar system into the simulation. After I reached Neptune, I had hit a wall. Due to the size of Neptune's orbit, it would not fit in the simulation window.

To counteract this issue, I started developing a camera that I could use to move around and zoom into the different planets. Slowly, this cycle of development kept going until I had added dwarf planets, moons, comets, and asteroids. I also integrated small features such as custom textures for the planets so that the simulation gave a better user experience.

One of the biggest challenges I ran into was balancing realism with computational performance. As I expanded the simulator to include the Asteroid Belt, comets, and the Kuiper Belt, the number of calculations grew exponentially. This issue forced me to think about efficiency. I experimented with optimization techniques, reorganized parts of the simulation loop, and separated physics calculations from rendering.

Perhaps the most satisfying milestone came when I successfully scaled portions of the simulation to handle over one million objects. To reach that point, I had to implement several optimization techniques. The process of getting there taught me that at certain scales, optimization is the true backbone of a programming project.

Although the simulator already models much of our solar system, I still consider it an ongoing project. In the future, I'd like to improve the numerical integration methods, simulate additional astronomical phenomena, incorporate spacecraft trajectories, and continue refining the user experience. Like many of my projects, I don't see it as something to finish; I see it as something that will continue growing as my own understanding grows.
